Long-lived assets

Long-lived assets are noncurrent. They are tangible or intangible.

  • Tangible assets have physical substance. They can be touched. Land, buildings, equipment.
  • Intangible assets have no physical substance and give the owner specific rights. Patents, trademarks, licenses, goodwill.

The operating cycle on the slides is: evaluate possible acquisitions, acquire the asset, estimate and recognize periodic depreciation or amortization, monitor value for impairment, and dispose of the asset.

Except for land, which is treated as having an unlimited life, a long-lived asset with a limited life is the prepaid cost of a bundle of future services. The expense-recognition (matching) principle puts a portion of that cost in the same periods as the revenues the asset helps earn. That allocation is depreciation for tangible assets and amortization for definite-life intangibles.

Acquisition cost

Under the cost principle, all reasonable and necessary expenditures to acquire the asset and prepare it for use are part of the asset’s cost. The slides list sales taxes, legal fees, transportation, and installation. Those amounts are capitalized. They are not expenses of the current period.

Interest on a purchase Interest charges associated with the purchase are recorded as expenses as incurred. This chapter does not capitalize borrowing costs into the asset.

Depreciation concepts

AccountDebitCredit
Depreciation expenseXX
Accumulated depreciationXX
 Accumulated depreciation is a contra-account of the related asset. It is not cash, and it is not a fund set aside to replace the asset.

Three inputs, once cost is known:

  1. Estimated useful life to this company.
  2. Estimated residual value (salvage value) at the end of that life.
  3. The depreciation method.

Different lives and residual values change reported profit, so two companies with similar assets can look different only because of these estimates. Land is not depreciated.

Depreciable cost = Cost − residual value

Net book value (carrying amount) = Cost − accumulated depreciation

Three methods · the delivery van

The worked schedules use a van that cost $62,500, with residual value $2,500, so depreciable cost is $60,000. Useful life is 3 years, ending 31 May. Estimated activity is 100,000 miles. One slide callout prints “Cost = $60,000” next to “depreciation expense = $20,000.” The schedule itself shows acquisition cost of $62,500 and depreciable cost of $60,000. Units of production

Depreciable cost is spread over estimated total output. Expense varies with actual use, so it tracks revenue when activity varies. It is a variable expense.

Unit rate = (Cost − residual) ÷ estimated total units

(62,500 − 2,500) / 100,000 miles = $0.60 per mile

Depreciation = unit rate × actual units this period

Double-declining balance

Chosen when the asset is more productive when it is newer: higher expense and higher revenues early, lower expense later. The rate is often twice the straight-line rate. The rate is applied to book value (cost minus accumulated depreciation), not to depreciable cost. Residual value is not in the formula until the end.

(Cost − accumulated depreciation) × 2 / useful life = depreciation expense

Straight-line rate = 1/3. Double-declining rate = 2/3.

Intangible assets

On acquisition, decide whether each intangible has a definite or an indefinite life.

  • Definite life. Allocate cost straight-line over useful life. That allocation is amortization, analogous to depreciation. Most companies estimate no residual value, because the asset will be used rather than sold. Amortization uses an accumulated-amortization contra-account in the lecture entries.
  • Indefinite life. Do not amortize. Review at least annually for impairment, starting with qualitative factors. An example of impairment is fair value below carrying amount. Goodwill is the indefinite-life case the slides develop.

Goodwill · Valley Drug

Wheeler pays £400,000 for Valley Drug. Fair values of the recorded items: inventory 220,000, long-term operating assets 110,000, other assets 10,000, liabilities 20,000. Identifiable net assets = (220,000 + 110,000 + 10,000) − 20,000 = 320,000. Goodwill = 400,000 − 320,000 = 80,000. The slides describe those 80,000 as the other favorable factors Wheeler was willing to pay for: reputation, service, location, management, financial standing. For accounting, goodwill is the excess of the purchase price of the company as a whole over the fair value of its identifiable net assets.

Impairment

An impairment test asks whether book value (carrying amount) is higher than recoverable amount. If it is, there is an impairment loss.

Recoverable amount = the higher of (fair value − disposal costs) and value in use

If carrying amount > recoverable amount, impairment loss = the difference

Value in use is the value of keeping the asset and generating cash. Fair value minus disposal costs is the value of selling it. Take the higher of those two, then compare with carrying amount.

Wheeler fitness-center building

Bought five years ago for £600,000. Straight-line, 20-year life, no residual value. Remaining life is still estimated at 15 years. There are indications of impairment. Present value of cash flows over the remaining 15 years (value in use) is £375,000. Net fair value is £230,000.

Annual depreciation = 600,000 / 20 = 30,000

Accumulated depreciation after 5 years = 30,000 × 5 = 150,000

Carrying amount = 600,000 − 150,000 = 450,000

Recoverable amount = higher of 230,000 and 375,000 = 375,000

Impairment loss = 450,000 − 375,000 = 75,000

Quiz 7.3 · Amber Company, worked

Building cost £900,000. Accumulated depreciation at 1 January of the current year is £270,000. Fair value £500,000. Direct selling cost £20,000. Value in use £500,000.

Carrying amount = 900,000 − 270,000 = 630,000

Fair value less costs to sell = 500,000 − 20,000 = 480,000

Recoverable amount = higher of 480,000 and 500,000 = 500,000

Impairment loss = 630,000 − 500,000 = 130,000

The building is impaired because 630,000 > 500,000. Debit impairment loss 130,000; credit accumulated impairment loss — building 130,000. Do not compare carrying amount with the raw fair value of 500,000 and forget the £20,000 selling cost when you identify recoverable amount. The selling cost matters, and value in use still wins.

Disposals

A disposal can be involuntary (storm, fire, theft, accident) or voluntary (drop a product, replace a machine with a more efficient one). Voluntary disposals in the slides include sales, trade-ins, and retirements. The entries that are actually worked are scrapping and sale.

A disposal of a depreciable asset usually needs two entries:

  1. Update depreciation expense and accumulated depreciation up to the disposal date.
  2. Record the disposal. Remove the cost and the accumulated depreciation. The difference between resources received and net book value is a gain or a loss, reported in operations on the income statement.

Gain (loss) = cash proceeds − book value − any cash paid to remove the asset

Book value = cost − accumulated depreciation, after the updating entry

Fixed asset turnover

The first learning outcome says to interpret the fixed asset turnover ratio. The slides name it and do not compute a company example, so there is no lecture number to memorize. The standard ratio that outcome refers to is:

Fixed asset turnover = Net sales ÷ Average net fixed assets

Average net fixed assets = (beginning net PPE + ending net PPE) ÷ 2

Net fixed assets means cost minus accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment. A higher ratio means more sales per unit of net PPE. Compare a firm with its own prior years or with similar firms. A company that has just bought a large asset, or that holds a lot of land, will look slower until those assets produce sales. Depreciation lowers the denominator, so the ratio can rise even when sales do not. The ratio is not a cash-flow measure. The cash effects are in the next section.

Cash-flow effects

The learning outcome asks for the cash effect of acquiring, using, and disposing of long-lived assets. The slides show that effect through the journal entries rather than through a statement of cash flows.

  • Acquisition for cash is a cash outflow (the truck, the lump-sum purchase, the license). Buying with a note is not a cash outflow for the financed portion. The asset and the liability both rise. Interest later is an operating cash outflow, and in this chapter it is not added to the asset.
  • Use — depreciation, amortization, and impairment loss reduce net income and do not use cash. Accumulated depreciation, accumulated amortization, and accumulated impairment loss are not cash accounts.
  • Disposal — the cash flow is the cash received, minus any cash paid to remove the asset. The gain or loss is the plug that makes the entry balance. A gain is not additional cash. A loss is not a cash payment. Selling the computer for £600 produces a £600 inflow whether the entry also shows a gain (after five years) or a loss (after four years).

Exam traps

  • Capitalize sales tax, legal fees, transport, and installation. Expense interest on the purchase as incurred. A cash discount reduces the capitalized cost (the truck is 15,096, not 15,396).
  • Allocate a lump-sum price by relative fair value, not by area or by the number of assets. Land 25% and building 75% come from 1,000,000 / 4,000,000 and 3,000,000 / 4,000,000.
  • Ordinary repairs are expenses. Improvements that extend life, efficiency, or capacity are added to the asset. Expensing lowers taxes now; capitalizing raises current assets and current income.
  • Land is not depreciated. Residual value is subtracted for straight-line and units-of-production. It is not subtracted inside the double-declining formula until the last year, when book value is not allowed to fall below it.
  • Double-declining multiplies beginning book value by twice the straight-line rate. It does not multiply depreciable cost by that rate. Year 1 of the van is 62,500 × 2/3, not 60,000 × 2/3.
  • Units-of-production expense changes every period with actual output. Straight-line does not.
  • Amortize a definite-life intangible over useful life when that is shorter than remaining legal life. The patent uses 8 years, not 13. An indefinite life, including goodwill and the auto-renewing license, is not amortized.
  • Goodwill = price paid − fair value of identifiable net assets, and net assets are assets minus liabilities. Forgetting to subtract the £20,000 of liabilities makes goodwill 60,000 instead of 80,000.
  • Recoverable amount is the higher of value in use and fair value less disposal costs. Compare carrying amount with that number. In both lecture cases, value in use is the higher one.
  • After impairment, depreciation restarts from the new carrying amount over the remaining life. Wheeler’s later depreciation is 25,000, not the old 30,000.
  • On a disposal, update depreciation first. Remove both cost and accumulated depreciation. Cash of 600 against a book value of 3,000 is a loss of 2,400, not a gain.
  • A loss on disposal is not the cash paid, unless book value is already zero and the only cash is a removal cost.

Practice

1. A company pays 900,000 for land and a building. Appraisal values are land 200,000 and building 600,000. The purchase is paid with 300,000 cash and a note for the rest. Record the purchase.

Total fair value = 800,000. Land = 200/800 × 900,000 = 225,000. Building = 600/800 × 900,000 = 675,000. Debit land 225,000; debit building 675,000; credit cash 300,000; credit note payable 600,000.

2. Equipment costs 48,000, residual value 3,000, life 5 years or 90,000 units. Year 1 output is 18,000 units. Compute year-1 depreciation under straight-line, units of production, and double-declining balance.

Straight-line: (48,000 − 3,000) / 5 = 9,000. Units: rate = 45,000 / 90,000 = 0.50 per unit; expense = 0.50 × 18,000 = 9,000. Double-declining rate = 2/5 = 40%. Year 1 = 48,000 × 0.40 = 19,200. Residual value is ignored in the first DDB year.

3. A building cost 400,000 and has been depreciated straight-line for 6 years on a 20-year life with no residual value. Value in use is now 250,000. Fair value is 220,000 and disposal costs are 10,000. Compute the impairment loss and next year’s depreciation. Remaining life is 14 years.

Accumulated depreciation = (400,000 / 20) × 6 = 120,000. Carrying amount = 280,000. Fair value less disposal costs = 210,000. Recoverable amount = higher of 210,000 and 250,000 = 250,000. Loss = 280,000 − 250,000 = 30,000. Debit impairment loss 30,000; credit accumulated impairment loss 30,000. Next depreciation = 250,000 / 14 = 17,857 (rounded to the nearest pound or dollar, as the question requires).

4. A machine cost 24,000, has accumulated depreciation of 18,000, and is sold for 4,000. Depreciation is already up to date. Record the sale. Separately, what is the entry if instead the machine is scrapped and removal costs 500 cash?

Book value = 6,000. Sale: debit cash 4,000; debit accumulated depreciation 18,000; debit loss 2,000; credit machine 24,000. Scrap with a 500 removal cost: debit accumulated depreciation 18,000; debit loss 6,500; credit machine 24,000; credit cash 500. The loss is the book value plus the cash paid to remove it.

5. A company pays 500,000 for another firm. Identifiable assets have a fair value of 460,000 and liabilities have a fair value of 70,000. How much is goodwill? The acquired firm also has a patent that cost the seller 40,000, with 12 years of legal life left and a useful life of 10 years, and no residual value. What is annual amortization, and what entry is made for goodwill at year-end?

Net identifiable assets = 460,000 − 70,000 = 390,000. Goodwill = 500,000 − 390,000 = 110,000. Patent amortization uses useful life: 40,000 / 10 = 4,000 per year. Debit amortization expense 4,000; credit accumulated amortization 4,000. Goodwill is not amortized. There is no year-end amortization entry. It is tested for impairment.
