Part 1 · Chapter 5

Cash and Receivables

Anything in Chapter 5 might be on the final. The slides do not print a formal learning-outcome list. These are the tasks the deck actually trains, including the end-of-chapter exercises.

  • State the three cash-management responsibilities.
  • Reconcile the bank balance to the book balance and journalize only the book side.
  • Record credit sales, cash collections, sales discounts, and sales returns, and compute net sales.
  • Estimate bad debts with the allowance method (percentage of receivables and aging), write off accounts, and reinstate later collections.
  • Record a note received in settlement of an account, interest at maturity, and a dishonored note.
  • Compute receivables turnover and the average collection period.

1. Cash

Cash-management responsibilities in the slides:

  1. Accurate accounting so cash-flow reports and cash balances can be prepared.
  2. Controls so enough cash is on hand for current operations, maturing liabilities, and unexpected emergencies.
  3. Avoid idle cash. Idle cash earns nothing, so surplus cash is invested in securities until operations need it.

Using a bank account is itself an internal control. Each month the bank statement shows the opening balance, deposits, checks processed, and the ending balance. That statement will not equal the company’s cash account, so the two are reconciled.

Reconciling items

Timing differences

Outstanding checks
Checks the company has already deducted, which the bank has not yet paid. Subtract them from the bank balance. A check voided when it was written is not outstanding.
Deposits in transit
Deposits the company has recorded, which the bank has not processed by the statement date. Add them to the bank balance.

Items the books do not yet show

  • Service charges — bank fees on the statement, not yet on the books. Expense.
  • NSF checks — a deposit that the bank reverses because the payer’s account had insufficient funds. The slides’ reconciliation labels this line “NFS”; that is the NSF item. It reduces book cash and restores a receivable.
  • Direct deposits — amounts the bank has already credited, often a customer paying the company.
  • Interest paid by the bank — interest on the company’s balance. Interest revenue.
  • Errors — either the bank or the company can mis-record a cash amount. Correct an error on the side that made it.
What gets a journal entry Deposits in transit and outstanding checks are already on the books. They change only the bank side of the reconciliation. Journal entries are required for items that update the company’s cash account: interest, direct deposits, service charges, NSF checks, bank-initiated payments, and company errors.

Correct cash = Bank balance + deposits in transit − outstanding checks ± bank errors

Correct cash = Book balance + interest and other unrecorded receipts − charges, NSF, and other unrecorded payments ± company errors

2. Receivables

A receivable is a claim for money, goods, or services.

Accounts receivable
A current asset for money due for services performed or merchandise sold on credit.
Notes receivable
A formal note written by the customer. A promissory note is a stronger legal claim than an open account.

The selling cycle in the slides runs: accept the order, deliver, collect cash, accept returns, deal with non-payment, and provide continued service (warranties). The chapter works the first five. Warranty expense and warranty liability are only named here; the entries are in Chapter 8.

AccountWhat it does
Sales discountsContra-revenue. A reduction of the selling price if the customer pays inside a stated period.
Sales returns and allowancesContra-revenue. Deducted from sales.
Allowance for bad debtsContra-receivable. Deducted from accounts receivable to reach the cash expected to be collected.

Gross sales and net sales

Gross sales = sales revenue before discounts and returns

Net sales = gross sales − sales discounts − sales returns and allowances

The lecture combines the bakery illustrations on one partial statement of comprehensive income. The discount is granted only on merchandise that is not returned. Of the 2,000 THB credit sale, 500 THB is returned, so the 2% discount applies to 1,500 THB, not to 2,000.

THB. Discount = 40 − (500 × 0.02) = 40 − 10 = 30. Net sales = 5,000 − 30 − 1,500 = 3,470.

Gross method, which is the method in every lecture entry Record the sale at the full invoice price. Record Sales discounts only when the customer actually pays inside the discount window. An invoice that is still unpaid at the period end is still in gross sales, with no discount subtracted yet.

Bad debts and the allowance method

The slides cross out the practice of waiting until a specific account is known to be uncollectible and then recording the loss. Accounting standards require an expected-loss model: recognize impairment of receivables before any particular debt goes bad. That is the allowance method.

Percentage of total receivables

This method sets the ending balance of the allowance. Bad debt expense is whatever entry is needed to reach that balance. Ending receivables 500,000 THB, estimated 12% uncollectible.

Target allowance = 500,000 × 0.12 = 60,000

Net receivables = 500,000 × 0.88 = 440,000

Bad debt expense = target allowance − existing credit balance in the allowance

Percentage of credit sales, Exercise 5 only The lecture illustrations above are balance-sheet methods: they force the allowance to a target. Exercise 5 uses a different method. Witz Electric has credit sales of $1,300,000 and estimates bad debts at 1% of credit sales. Expense is 1,300,000 × 0.01 = $13,000, debited to bad debt expense and credited to the allowance, without looking at the existing allowance balance. A $4,000 write-off is still debit allowance, credit accounts receivable. Do not mix the two methods in one computation.

Aging accounts receivable

Each receivable is sorted by how long it has been outstanding. Older buckets get higher loss percentages. The sum of (bucket balance × percentage) is the required ending allowance. Expense is again only the amount needed to reach that balance.

Notes receivable

Four facts on the note: face amount (the amount written on the note), issuance date, due date (maturity date), and the interest rate, which is stated on an annual basis.

Interest = face × annual rate × time

In the lecture example, a 90-day note is measured as 3/12 of a year, not 90/365.

On 1 May 2017 HoHo accepts a 90-day, 12% note with face £5,000 to settle CoMo’s open account. Maturity on the slide is 30 July 2017. Interest = 5,000 × 0.12 × 3/12 = £150. Cash due at maturity = 5,000 + 150 = £5,150.

Receivables turnover

Analytical question on the slide: how effective are credit-granting and collection activities?

Accounts receivable turnover = Sales revenue ÷ Average accounts receivable

Average collection period = 365 ÷ Receivables turnover

The numerator in the slides is sales revenue, not “net credit sales.” A higher turnover means faster collection. Cash comes back sooner, so it can be invested or used to reduce borrowing. The average collection period is how long a customer takes to pay.

Skechers U.S.A. turnover in the comparison box: 2021 = 9.33, 2022 = 9.42, 2023 = 9.36. Average collection period for 2023 = 365 / 9.36 = 39.0 days.

Comparison caution, from the slide Compare a firm only with its own prior years, or with other firms in the same industry that use the same financing practices.

Lecture exercises on net sales, worked

These three problems are printed at the end of the chapter. Count the days from the invoice date. A discount is recorded only if collection falls inside the discount window, and only on goods the customer kept.

Exercise 1 · Hancock, two months ended 28 February

  • 6 Jan: S. Green, $1,500, terms 2/10, n/30. Collected 14 Jan, eight days later. Discount = 1,500 × 0.02 = $30.
  • 6 Jan: M. Munoz, $850, terms 2/10, n/30. Collected 2 Feb, well after ten days. No discount.
  • 28 Feb: R. Reynolds, $400, terms 2/10, n/45. Still unpaid when the period ends. No discount yet. The $400 is still in gross sales.

Gross sales = 1,500 + 850 + 400 = 2,750

Net sales = 2,750 − 30 = 2,720

The $60 card fee is not subtracted here. It is selling, general, and administrative expense.

Exam traps

  • Journalizing deposits in transit or outstanding checks. They are already recorded. Only the book side of the reconciliation is journalized.
  • A voided check is not an outstanding check.
  • Direction of a company error. Recording a $420 wage check as $240 overstates cash by $180. The correction credits cash.
  • A customer’s direct deposit clears a receivable. The Hunt entry credits accounts receivable, not sales.
  • Sales discounts and sales returns are contra-revenue. The allowance for bad debts is a contra-asset. A credit-card fee is an operating expense and is not deducted in net sales.
  • Under the gross method, do not subtract a discount on an invoice the customer has not yet paid. Hancock’s 28 February sale of $400 is still in gross sales in full.
  • After a partial return, the discount rate applies only to the goods kept. Bennett’s discount is 3% of $8,100, not 3% of $9,000.
  • Count the days. A collection on day 30 of an n/30 invoice is not inside a 10-day discount window.
  • After a return, the cash discount applies only to goods the customer keeps. In the bakery statement the discount is 30, not 40, and not 40 + 10.
  • A write-off does not change net receivables and does not record bad debt expense.
  • Under percentage-of-receivables or aging, expense equals the gap between the required allowance and the allowance balance already on the books. Subtract write-offs before you measure that gap. In the second aging example the expense is 19,700, not 35,294 and not 35,294 − 29,700.
  • A debit balance in the allowance (write-offs larger than the old allowance) is added to the target, not subtracted. The slides illustrate credit balances; the arithmetic is the same rule.
  • Percentage of credit sales (Exercise 5) ignores the existing allowance. Percentage of receivables and aging do not.
  • A dishonored note still produces interest revenue. The claim moves to accounts receivable at face plus interest.
  • For the 90-day note, the slides use 3/12, which equals 90/360. Do not switch to 90/365 unless the question says so.
  • Receivables turnover on this exam uses sales revenue ÷ average receivables, then 365 ÷ that turnover.

Practice

1. Bank reconciliation. Bank balance $8,400. Book balance $7,650. Deposit in transit $1,200. Outstanding checks $900. Interest earned by the bank $80. A customer’s electronic payment of $1,200 is on the bank statement and is not yet on the books. Service charge $30. NSF check $140. A collection from a customer was recorded as $560 instead of $500. What is the correct cash balance, and what entries update the books?

Bank: 8,400 + 1,200 − 900 = 8,700. The recording error overstated cash by 60. Book: 7,650 + 80 + 1,200 − 30 − 140 − 60 = 8,700.

Debit cash 1,280; credit interest revenue 80; credit accounts receivable 1,200. Debit miscellaneous expense 30; debit accounts receivable 140 (NSF); debit accounts receivable 60 (the collection was recorded 60 too high, so the receivable was reduced too far); credit cash 230.

Do not journalize the deposit in transit or the outstanding checks.

2. Net sales. Gross sales are 8,000, of which 5,000 is on credit, terms 2/10, n/30. Customers return 1,000 of the credit sales before paying. The rest of the credit customers pay inside the discount period. What is net sales?

Discount base = 5,000 − 1,000 = 4,000. Discount = 4,000 × 0.02 = 80. Net sales = 8,000 − 80 − 1,000 = 7,920. The cash sales of 3,000 are not discounted and were not returned.

3. Aging. Not yet due 200,000 at 2%. Past due 40,000 at 10%. Over 90 days 10,000 at 40%. The allowance has a credit balance of 3,000 before the adjustment, after write-offs. Prepare the adjusting entry and state net receivables.

Required allowance = 200,000 × 0.02 + 40,000 × 0.10 + 10,000 × 0.40 = 4,000 + 4,000 + 4,000 = 12,000. Expense = 12,000 − 3,000 = 9,000. Debit bad debt expense 9,000; credit allowance 9,000. Receivables total 250,000. Net receivables = 250,000 − 12,000 = 238,000.

4. A 120-day, 9% note with face 20,000 is accepted on 1 March to settle an account. Use a 30-day month, as the lecture uses months for its 90-day note (120 days = 4 months). Record acceptance, and then the entry if the maker pays at maturity. What changes if the note is dishonored?

Interest = 20,000 × 0.09 × 4/12 = 600. Acceptance: debit notes receivable 20,000; credit accounts receivable 20,000. Collection: debit cash 20,600; credit notes receivable 20,000; credit interest revenue 600. If dishonored: debit accounts receivable 20,600; credit notes receivable 20,000; credit interest revenue 600. Interest revenue is recognized either way.

5. Receivables are 80,000 and the allowance is 6,000 credit. An account of 2,000 is written off. What is net receivables before and after the write-off? A week later, 500 of that account is collected. Give both recovery entries.

Before: 80,000 − 6,000 = 74,000. After: receivables 78,000, allowance 4,000, net still 74,000. No bad debt expense on the write-off. Recovery: debit accounts receivable 500, credit allowance 500; then debit cash 500, credit accounts receivable 500.
