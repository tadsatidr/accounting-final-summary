Inventory

Learning outcomes printed on the first slide:

  • Decide when the use of different inventory costing methods is beneficial to a company.

  • Understand methods for controlling inventory and analyze the effects of inventory errors on financial statements.
  • Evaluate inventory management using the inventory turnover ratio and analyze the effects of inventory on cash flows.

The chapter map has five blocks: nature of inventory and cost of goods sold; inventory costing methods; lower of cost or NRV; control of inventory; evaluating inventory management.

Why the inventory records exist

The accounting system has three jobs in inventory management. It supplies numbers for periodic financial statements and tax returns. It supplies up-to-date quantities and costs for ordering and manufacturing. And, because inventory can be stolen or misused, it supplies the information needed to protect the asset.

Inventory is a current asset: it is normally used or turned into cash within one year or the next operating cycle.

Perpetual and periodic · Grantsville Clothing

Same transactions, two sets of accounts. Under a perpetual system, inventory and cost of goods sold are updated as goods move. Under a periodic system, purchases, freight-in, purchase returns, and purchase discounts are temporary accounts. Cost of goods sold is computed only after a physical count. Grantsville, on the periodic system, would know total sales of $23,000 and would not know, from the accounts alone, how many shirts and pairs of pants had been sold.

Cash is not miscounted by an inventory error of this kind. Income and the inventory asset are. That is the cash-flow point attached to this error: the accounting income moves, the cash collected from customers does not.

What cost includes, and how it flows

Goods are first recorded at cost. Cost includes the invoice price plus other expenditures to get the goods ready, such as freight-in and inspection and preparation costs. Purchase returns, allowances, and purchase discounts are subtracted.

For a merchandiser, the purchase increases inventory (or Purchases, if periodic). The sale increases cost of goods sold and decreases inventory.

Cost of goods sold = beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory

Harley-Davidson illustration for Motorclothes: beginning inventory $40,000, purchases $55,000, ending inventory $35,000. Cost of goods sold = 40,000 + 55,000 − 35,000 = $60,000.

Manufacturer flow

  1. Buy raw materials: debit raw materials inventory, credit cash or accounts payable.
  2. Use them. Direct materials, direct labor, and factory overhead move into work in process. Direct labor is the earnings of employees who work directly on the product. Factory overhead is all other manufacturing cost. Debit work in process; credit raw materials, cash or wages payable, and supplies (overhead).
  3. Finish the goods: debit finished goods, credit work in process.
  4. Sell them: debit cost of goods sold, credit finished goods. The same equation still computes cost of goods sold.

Four costing methods

The four methods on the slide are specific identification, first-in first-out (FIFO), last-in first-out (LIFO), and average cost. The choice is a cost-flow assumption. It is not a description of which physical unit left the shelf.

  • Specific identification tracks the purchase cost of each item. It is impractical when large quantities of similar items are stocked. The slides give no numerical example.
  • FIFO treats the earliest goods purchased as the first goods sold. Oldest unit costs go to cost of goods sold. Newest unit costs stay in ending inventory.
  • LIFO does the reverse: newest costs go to cost of goods sold, oldest costs stay in ending inventory. IFRS does not allow LIFO. The comparison table prints a LIFO column and then crosses the heading out for that reason. The arithmetic is still in the chapter, so it is below.
  • Average cost uses one weighted-average unit cost for both cost of goods sold and ending inventory.

Weighted average unit cost = Cost of goods available for sale ÷ Number of units available for sale

Managers in the slides choose a method for two reasons:

  1. Net income. Managers prefer to report higher earnings. In this rising-cost example, FIFO reports the highest income ($75) and the highest inventory.
  2. Income tax, the “least–latest” rule. Managers prefer to pay the smallest tax the law allows, as late as possible. In this example LIFO reports the lowest tax ($15). IFRS removes that option by prohibiting LIFO.

If prices were falling, the income ranking would reverse. The slides do not work a falling-price case; they work this one.

Cash paid to suppliers does not depend on the cost-flow assumption. The method changes cost of goods sold, income, tax, and the inventory asset. The cash-flow effect of the choice runs through the tax payment, not through the supplier payment.

Lower of cost or net realizable value

Inventory starts at purchase cost, under the cost principle. If net realizable value falls below cost, the units are reported at NRV. The loss is recognized in the period the value drops, not in the period the item is sold. The slides tie that departure from cost to the conservatism constraint: take care not to overstate assets and income.

NRV = sales price − costs to sell

Inventory turnover and days’ sales in inventory

Analytical question: how efficient are inventory management activities? The ratio is how many times average inventory was produced and sold during the period. A higher ratio means inventory moves faster to the customer, which cuts storage and obsolescence costs.

Inventory turnover = Cost of goods sold ÷ Average inventory

Average inventory = (Beginning inventory + Ending inventory) ÷ 2

Number of days’ sales in inventory = 365 ÷ Inventory turnover

Days’ sales in inventory is the average time to produce and deliver inventory to customers. The slide’s comparison:

Exam traps

  • Perpetual purchase returns and purchase discounts reduce Inventory. Periodic returns and discounts go to Purchase returns and Purchase discounts. Freight is Inventory under perpetual and Freight-in under periodic.
  • Periodic systems do not record cost of goods sold on the day of sale. Forgetting the second perpetual entry (the cost entry) is equally common.
  • The $194 shirt discount is 2% of $9,700, the amount after the return, not 2% of the original $10,000.
  • Shrinkage gets its own entry only in a perpetual system. In a periodic system it is buried inside cost of goods sold.
  • An ending-inventory error reverses next year. It does not permanently raise income, and it does not change net purchases in the Salina table.
  • FIFO and LIFO describe cost flow, not the physical pile. You can sell the newest unit and still use FIFO.
  • IFRS does not allow LIFO. If the question is under IFRS, FIFO or average are the available assumptions from this chapter. The printed LIFO column is crossed out for that reason.
  • Weighted average uses units available, not units sold, as the denominator. Then that one unit cost is applied to both the units sold and the units left.
  • NRV is selling price minus costs to sell, not selling price alone. The car’s NRV is 15,500, not 16,000.
  • Do not write inventory up when NRV exceeds cost. Items A, B, and C stay at historical cost. Only D is reduced.
  • The write-down in this chapter debits cost of goods sold and credits an allowance, a contra-inventory account.
  • Turnover uses cost of goods sold, not sales. Days on hand uses 365 ÷ turnover, not turnover ÷ 365.

Practice

1. Beginning inventory is 10 units at $4. Purchases are 20 units at $5 and 10 units at $7. Sales are 25 units. Compute ending inventory and cost of goods sold under FIFO, LIFO, and weighted average.

Units available = 40. Cost available = 40 + 100 + 70 = 210. Ending units = 15.

FIFO ending = 10×7 + 5×5 = 70 + 25 = 95. COGS = 210 − 95 = 115 (10×4 + 15×5).

LIFO ending = 10×4 + 5×5 = 40 + 25 = 65. COGS = 210 − 65 = 145 (10×7 + 15×5).

Average unit cost = 210 / 40 = 5.25. Ending = 15 × 5.25 = 78.75. COGS = 25 × 5.25 = 131.25.

2. Three items, item by item. A: cost 40, selling price 50, cost to sell 6. B: cost 20, selling price 22, cost to sell 1. C: cost 15, selling price 14, cost to sell 0. What inventory total is reported, and what is the write-down entry?

NRV: A = 50 − 6 = 44, so report 40. B = 22 − 1 = 21, so report 20. C = 14, so report 14. Total = 74. Cost was 75, so the write-down is 1. Debit cost of goods sold 1; credit allowance for inventory write-down 1. A is not written up from 40 to 44.

3. Ending inventory of year 1 is understated by 3,000. Purchases and sales are otherwise correct, and year 2 ending inventory is correct. What happens to cost of goods sold and net income in each year?

Year 1: COGS overstated 3,000, net income understated 3,000. Year 2: beginning inventory understated 3,000, COGS understated 3,000, net income overstated 3,000. The two income errors offset. Net purchases are unchanged in both years.

4. A perpetual inventory record shows $48,000. The physical count is $46,400. Give the shrinkage entry. What entry would a periodic system make for the same shortage?

Perpetual: debit cost of goods sold 1,600; credit inventory 1,600. Periodic: no shrinkage entry. The count of 46,400 is ending inventory, and the shortage is inside the single cost-of-goods-sold computation (goods available − 46,400).

5. Cost of goods sold is 420,000. Beginning inventory 70,000. Ending inventory 90,000. Compute turnover and days’ sales in inventory.

Average inventory = (70,000 + 90,000) / 2 = 80,000. Turnover = 420,000 / 80,000 = 5.25 times. Days = 365 / 5.25 = 69.5 days.
